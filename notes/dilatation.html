1. Preliminaries on differentiability

1.1 Partial derivatives and the classes $C^k$

Definition 1.1 Let $f : A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$. The $i$-th partial derivative of $f$ at $x$ is $$ \partial_i f(x) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(x + t e_i) - f(x)}{t}, $$ where $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector.

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and nonempty.

  • $C^0(U, \mathbb{R}^m)$ denotes the set of continuous functions $U \to \mathbb{R}^m$.
  • For $k \geq 1$, $$ C^k(U,\mathbb{R}^m) = \Big\{ f \in C^0(U,\mathbb{R}^m) \;\Big|\; \text{all partials } \partial_{i_1}\partial_{i_2}\cdots\partial_{i_l} f \text{ exist and are continuous on } U \Big\}, $$ where $1 \le l \le k$. For such $f$ the order of differentiation is immaterial: $\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_l} f = \partial_{i_l}\cdots\partial_{i_1} f$.
  • $C^\infty(U,\mathbb{R}^m) = \bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty} C^k(U,\mathbb{R}^m)$.
  • For $0 \le k \le \infty$, $C_0^k(U,\mathbb{R}^m) = \{ f \in C^k(U,\mathbb{R}^m) \mid \operatorname{supp}(f) \text{ is a compact subset of } U \}$.

1.2 The Wirtinger derivatives

From $z = x+iy$ and $\bar z = x-iy$ we may solve for the real coordinates, $x = \tfrac{z+\bar z}{2}$, $y = \tfrac{z-\bar z}{2i}$, and if we pretend $z,\bar z$ are independent variables replacing $x,y$, the chain rule gives

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} = \frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} - i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big) = \partial, $$

and similarly $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial \bar z} = \dfrac12\Big(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} + i\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big) = \bar\partial$.

Identifying $\mathbb{C}$ with $\mathbb{R}^2$ by $z = x+iy$, define

$$ \partial = \tfrac12(\partial_1 - i\partial_2) = \tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x} - i\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big), \qquad \bar\partial = \tfrac12(\partial_1 + i\partial_2) = \tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x} + i\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big). $$

In this notation the Cauchy–Riemann equation is simply $\bar\partial f = 0$, so $f$ is holomorphic precisely when its $\bar\partial$-derivative vanishes.

1.3 Differentiability in complex form

Let $f : A \to \mathbb{C}$ with $A \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, and let $T(h) = \lim_{t\to 0} \dfrac{f(z+th)-f(z)}{t}$ be the derivative of $f$ at $z$, regarded as an $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$.

Theorem 1.1 $$ T(h) = \partial f(z)\, h \;+\; \bar\partial f(z)\, \bar h. $$
Proof. Taking $h=1$, $T(1) = \lim_{t\to 0} \dfrac{f(z+t)-f(z)}{t} = f_x$, and similarly $T(i) = f_y$. Since $T$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear, writing $h = h_1 + ih_2$ with $h_1,h_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ gives $T(h) = h_1 T(1) + h_2 T(i) = h_1 f_x + h_2 f_y$. Now from $h = h_1+ih_2$ and $\bar h = h_1 - ih_2$, $h_1 = \tfrac{h+\bar h}{2}$, $h_2 = \tfrac{h-\bar h}{2i}$, so $$ T(h) = \Big(\frac{h+\bar h}{2}\Big) f_x + \Big(\frac{h-\bar h}{2i}\Big) f_y. $$ Using $\tfrac{1}{2i} = -\tfrac{i}{2}$ and collecting the coefficients of $h$ and $\bar h$, $$ T(h) = \tfrac12(f_x - if_y)\,h + \tfrac12(f_x+if_y)\,\bar h = \partial f(z)\,h + \bar\partial f(z)\,\bar h. $$ ∎

1.4 Standard theorems

Theorem 1.2 (Chain rule; 2.8.1) Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$. If $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable at a point $x$ and $g : B \to \mathbb{R}^p$ is differentiable at $y = f(x)$, then the composite $g \circ f : A \cap f^{-1}(B) \to \mathbb{R}^p$ is differentiable at $x$, and its derivative matrix satisfies $(g\circ f)'(x) = g'(y)\, f'(x)$.
Theorem 1.3 (Differentiability of the inverse; 2.8.2) Let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be injective. Suppose $f$ is continuous in some neighbourhood of a point $x$, that $f$ is differentiable at $x$ itself, and that $f'(x)$ is nonsingular. Then $y = f(x)$ is an interior point of $f(A)$, the function $g = f^{-1} : f(A) \to A$ is differentiable at $y$, and $g'(y) = f'(x)^{-1}$.
Theorem 1.4 (Inverse function theorem; 2.8.3) Let $U$ be open in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $f \in C^k(U,\mathbb{R}^n)$ with $1 \le k \le \infty$. If $x \in U$ is a point at which the Jacobian satisfies $J_f(x) \ne 0$, then there exists $r>0$ such that the restriction of $f$ to the ball $B = B_n(x,r)$ is a $C^k$-diffeomorphism of $B$ onto $f(B)$.

2. Stretching of a linear map

A linear map does not scale all directions equally: a unit vector may be lengthened in one direction and shortened in another. The two extreme factors are the maximal and minimal stretching.

Definition 2.1 Let $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be linear. Define $$ L(T) = \max_{|H|=1} |TH|, \qquad \ell(T) = \min_{|H|=1} |TH|. $$ Both extrema are attained: $H \mapsto |TH|$ is continuous and the unit sphere $S^{n-1}$ is compact. By homogeneity, for every $H \in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\ell(T)|H| \le |TH| \le L(T)|H|$, so $L(T)$ is the operator norm of $T$, and $\ell(T) > 0$ iff $T$ is injective.

2.1 Singular values via the spectral theorem

The matrix $T^*T$ is symmetric.

Theorem 2.1 (Spectral theorem) A real symmetric transformation has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

Let $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n$ be the eigenvalues of $T^*T$, with orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1,\dots,v_n$. Then each $\lambda_i \ge 0$, because $$ \lambda_i = \lambda_i \langle v_i,v_i\rangle = \langle \lambda_i v_i, v_i\rangle = \langle T^*T v_i, v_i\rangle = \langle Tv_i, Tv_i\rangle = |Tv_i|^2 \ge 0. $$ Hence $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$; writing $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$ gives $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0$, the singular values of $T$ ($\sigma_n > 0$ exactly when $T$ is nonsingular).

Proposition 2.2 For any $H$, $|TH|^2 = \langle T^*T H, H\rangle$, so by the Rayleigh quotient characterisation $$ L(T) = \sigma_1, \qquad \ell(T) = \sigma_n. $$ Moreover the extreme directions are orthogonal, being eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $T^*T$.

2.2 Geometric picture

$T$ carries the unit sphere of $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto an ellipsoid whose semi-axes have lengths $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n$. Thus $L(T)$ is the longest semi-axis and $\ell(T)$ the shortest: the ellipsoid is contained in the ball of radius $L(T)$ and contains the ball of radius $\ell(T)$.

3. Dilatations

Throughout this section $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is linear and nonsingular, so $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n > 0$ and $|\det T| = \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \cdots \sigma_n$.

Definition 3.1 The linear dilatation, inner dilatation, and outer dilatation of $T$ are $$ H(T) = \frac{L(T)}{\ell(T)}, \qquad H_I(T) = \frac{|\det T|}{\ell(T)^n}, \qquad H_O(T) = \frac{L(T)^n}{|\det T|}. $$ In terms of singular values, $$ H(T) = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_n}, \qquad H_I(T) = \prod_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_n}, \qquad H_O(T) = \prod_{i=2}^{n} \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_i}. $$ Geometrically, $H_I$ compares the volume of the image ellipsoid with that of its inscribed ball (radius $\ell(T)$), while $H_O$ compares the circumscribed ball (radius $L(T)$) with the ellipsoid. Since every factor in the two products lies in $[1, H(T)]$, all three quantities are $\ge 1$.

3.1 Dilatation and eccentricity

Writing $b = L(T)$ and $a = \ell(T)$ for the major and minor semi-axes in the plane, $H(T) = b/a$, and the eccentricity of the image ellipse is $$ e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{a^2}{b^2}} = \sqrt{1 - H(T)^{-2}}. $$ So $H(T)=1$ means $e=0$ (a circle: $T$ is a similarity, i.e. conformal), and $H(T)\to\infty$ means $e\to 1$.

3.2 The product identity

Proposition 3.1 $$ H_I(T)\cdot H_O(T) = H(T)^n, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad H_I(T) = \frac{H(T)^n}{H_O(T)}. $$
Proof. Multiply and cancel the determinant: $$ H_O(T)\cdot H_I(T) = \frac{L(T)^n}{|\det T|}\cdot\frac{|\det T|}{\ell(T)^n} = \frac{L(T)^n}{\ell(T)^n} = H(T)^n. $$ ∎

3.3 Comparison inequalities

Lemma 3.2 $H_O(T) \ge H(T)$.
Proof. Write $H_O(T) = \dfrac{\sigma_1^n}{\sigma_1\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_n} = \dfrac{\sigma_1^{n-1}}{\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_n}$. Since $\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_{n-1} \le \sigma_1^{n-2}$ (each factor $\le \sigma_1$), $$ H_O(T) \ge \frac{\sigma_1^{n-1}}{\sigma_1^{n-2}\cdot\sigma_n} = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_n} = H(T). \qquad \blacksquare $$
Corollary 3.3 $1 \le H_O(T) \le H_I(T)^{n-1}$ and $1 \le H_I(T) \le H_O(T)^{n-1}$.
Proof. By Proposition 3.1 and Lemma 3.2, $$ 1 \le H_I(T) = \frac{H(T)^n}{H_O(T)} \le \frac{H_O(T)^n}{H_O(T)} = H_O(T)^{n-1}, $$ and the other inequality follows by the symmetric argument. ∎
Theorem 3.4 $$ 1 \;\le\; \min\{H_I(T), H_O(T)\} \;\le\; H(T)^{n/2} \;\le\; \max\{H_I(T), H_O(T)\} \;\le\; H(T)^{n-1}. $$
Proof. (1) The middle two inequalities. Suppose, for contradiction, that both $H_I(T) > H(T)^{n/2}$ and $H_O(T) > H(T)^{n/2}$. Multiplying gives $H_I(T)\cdot H_O(T) > H(T)^n$, contradicting Proposition 3.1. So at least one of $H_I(T), H_O(T)$ is $\le H(T)^{n/2}$, i.e. the minimum is. The same argument with both inequalities reversed shows at least one is $\ge H(T)^{n/2}$, i.e. the maximum is.

(2) The last inequality. From the product formulas, $$ H_O(T) = \prod_{i=2}^n \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_i} \le H(T)^{n-1}, \qquad H_I(T) = \prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_n} \le H(T)^{n-1}, $$ because each factor is at most $\sigma_1/\sigma_n = H(T)$. Hence $1 \le \max\{H_O(T),H_I(T)\} \le H(T)^{n-1}$. ∎

The case $n=2$

For $n=2$ the three dilatations coincide: $$ H_I(T) = \frac{\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_2^2} = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}, \qquad H_O(T) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_1\sigma_2} = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}, $$ so $H_I(T) = H_O(T) = H(T)$, consistent with Theorem 3.4, whose outer bounds both read $H(T)^1$ when $n=2$.

4. Maximal and minimal stretching of a map

Definition 4.1 Let $f : A \to \mathbb{R}^m$. The maximal stretching $L_f(x)$ and minimal stretching $\ell_f(x)$ of $f$ at $x$ are $$ L_f(x) = \limsup_{h \to 0} \frac{|f(x+h)-f(x)|}{|h|}, \qquad \ell_f(x) = \liminf_{h \to 0} \frac{|f(x+h)-f(x)|}{|h|}. $$
Theorem 4.1 If $f$ is differentiable at $x$, then $L_f(x) = L(f'(x))$ and $\ell_f(x) = \ell(f'(x))$.
Proof. Differentiability gives $f(x+h)-f(x) = f'(x)h + |h|\varepsilon(h)$ with $\varepsilon(h)\to 0$ as $h\to 0$. Hence $$ |f(x+h)-f(x)| = |f'(x)h + |h|\varepsilon(h)| \le |h|L(f'(x)) + |h|\cdot\varepsilon(h), $$ so $\dfrac{|f(x+h)-f(x)|}{|h|} \le L(f'(x)) + \varepsilon(h) \xrightarrow{h\to 0} L(f'(x))$, giving $L_f(x) \le L(f'(x))$. For the reverse inequality, select $h \in S^{n-1}$ for which the maximum is attained, i.e. $L(f'(x)) = |f'(x)h|$. Approaching along that direction, $$ L_f(x) \ge \lim_{t\to 0^+} \frac{|f(x+th)-f(x)|}{t} = |f'(x)h| = L(f'(x)). $$ The two inequalities give $L_f(x) = L(f'(x))$; the statement for $\ell_f$ follows by the same argument with the inequalities reversed, choosing $h$ to minimise $|f'(x)h|$. ∎

Remark. This theorem is what makes concrete computations possible: an awkward $\limsup$ of difference quotients for a nonlinear $f$ is replaced by maximising $|TH|$ over the unit sphere, with $T = f'(x)$ a fixed matrix. In $\mathbb{R}^2$ one writes $H = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and the problem becomes an elementary one-variable trigonometric extremum — the method used in both examples below.

4.1 The complex case

Theorem 4.2 Let $f : A \to \mathbb{C}$ with $A \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, differentiable at $z$. Then $$ L_f(z) = |\partial f(z)| + |\bar\partial f(z)|, \qquad \ell_f(z) = \big| |\partial f(z)| - |\bar\partial f(z)| \big|. $$
Proof. By Theorem 4.1, $L_f(z) = L(f'(z))$, and by Theorem 1.1 the derivative is $T(h) = \partial f(z)\,h + \bar\partial f(z)\,\bar h$. Since $|\bar h| = |h|$, the triangle inequality (in both directions) gives $$ \big||\partial f(z)| - |\bar\partial f(z)|\big|\,|h| \;\le\; |T(h)| \;\le\; \big(|\partial f(z)| + |\bar\partial f(z)|\big)|h|. $$ Both bounds are attained. Taking $h = e^{i\theta}$ on the unit circle, $$ T(e^{i\theta}) = \partial f(z)e^{i\theta} + \bar\partial f(z)e^{-i\theta} = e^{i\theta}\big(\partial f(z) + \bar\partial f(z) e^{-2i\theta}\big), $$ so $|T(e^{i\theta})| = |\partial f(z) + \bar\partial f(z) e^{-2i\theta}|$. As $\theta$ runs over $[0,2\pi)$ the factor $e^{-2i\theta}$ runs over the whole unit circle, so the two complex numbers $\partial f(z)$ and $\bar\partial f(z) e^{-2i\theta}$ become aligned for some $\theta$ and anti-aligned for another. Hence the modulus attains both the sum and the absolute difference of the two moduli — $L(f'(z))$ and $\ell(f'(z))$ respectively. ∎

Remark. Theorem 4.2 makes the conformality criterion transparent: $H(f'(z)) = 1$ forces $|\bar\partial f(z)| = 0$, i.e. the Cauchy–Riemann equation. Example 1 below is the special case $\partial f = a$, $\bar\partial f = b$.

5. Example 1

Let $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $f(z) = az + b\bar z$. Identifying $\mathbb{C}$ with $\mathbb{R}^2$ via $z=x+iy$, so $z \leftrightarrow (x,y)$ and $\bar z \leftrightarrow (x,-y)$: $$ f(x,y) = a(x,y) + b(x,-y) = \big((a+b)x,\, (a-b)y\big), \qquad f\binom{x}{y} = \begin{pmatrix} a+b & 0 \\ 0 & a-b\end{pmatrix}\binom{x}{y} = AX. $$

Stretching of the derivative

Since $f$ is linear, it is its own derivative: $f'(X) = A = \begin{pmatrix} a+b & 0 \\ 0 & a-b \end{pmatrix}$. $A$ is diagonal, so by Proposition 2.2 its singular values are $|a+b|$ and $|a-b|$, hence $$ L(f'(X)) = \max\{|a+b|,|a-b|\}, \qquad \ell(f'(X)) = \min\{|a+b|,|a-b|\}. $$

Stretching of the map

Let $H = (h,k)$. By linearity of $f$, $$ \chi := \frac{|f(X+H)-f(X)|}{|H|} = \frac{|AH|}{|H|} = \frac{\sqrt{(a+b)^2h^2 + (a-b)^2k^2}}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}, $$ so $\chi^2 = \dfrac{(a+b)^2h^2+(a-b)^2k^2}{h^2+k^2}$. Let $h=r\cos\theta$, $k=r\sin\theta$. Then $$ \chi^2 = (a+b)^2(1-\sin^2\theta) + (a-b)^2\sin^2\theta = (a+b)^2 + \big[(a-b)^2-(a+b)^2\big]\sin^2\theta, $$ and since $(a-b)^2-(a+b)^2 = -4ab$, $$ \chi^2 = (a+b)^2 - 4ab\sin^2\theta. $$ Note $\chi$ does not depend on $r$, so the $\limsup$ as $r\to 0$ is simply the supremum over $\theta$.

When $\sin^2\theta = 1$: $\chi^2 = (a+b)^2-4ab = (a-b)^2$, so $\chi = |a-b|$.
When $\sin^2\theta = 0$: $\chi^2 = (a+b)^2$, so $\chi = |a+b|$.

So the two extreme values are $|a+b|$ and $|a-b|$, giving $$ L_f(X) = \max\{|a+b|,|a-b|\} = |a|+|b|, \qquad \ell_f(X) = \min\{|a+b|,|a-b|\} = \big||a|-|b|\big|, $$ which coincide with $L(f'(X))$ and $\ell(f'(X))$ found above — Theorem 4.1 is satisfied. $\blacksquare$

Consistency with the complex formula

For $f(z) = az+b\bar z$ we have $\partial f = a$ and $\bar\partial f = b$, so Theorem 4.2 gives directly $L_f(z) = |a|+|b|$, $\ell_f(z) = \big||a|-|b|\big|$, in agreement with the direct computation. The dilatation is $$ H(f'(z)) = \frac{|a|+|b|}{\big||a|-|b|\big|}, $$ which equals $1$ exactly when $b=0$, i.e. $f(z)=az$ is holomorphic — as $\bar\partial f = b = 0$ predicts.

Remark on the sign of $ab$ Which of $|a+b|,|a-b|$ is the maximum depends on the sign of $ab$, since $-4ab$ is the coefficient of $\sin^2\theta$: $|a+b|^2 - |a-b|^2 = 4ab$.
  • If $ab \ge 0$: $\chi^2$ decreases in $\sin^2\theta$, so $L_f(X) = |a+b|$, $\ell_f(X) = |a-b|$.
  • If $ab < 0$: $\chi^2$ increases in $\sin^2\theta$ and the two swap: $L_f(X) = |a-b|$, $\ell_f(X)=|a+b|$.
The forms $|a|+|b|$ and $\big||a|-|b|\big|$ boxed above are valid in both cases, and remain valid for complex $a,b$ by Theorem 4.2.

6. Example 2

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be given by $f(x,y) = (x+y,\, x-y,\, xy)$. Find the maximum and minimum stretching, and verify $L_f(x) = L(f'(x))$.

Solution

$f$ is differentiable since every component is. Its derivative is $$ f'(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f_1/\partial x & \partial f_1/\partial y \\ \partial f_2/\partial x & \partial f_2/\partial y \\ \partial f_3/\partial x & \partial f_3/\partial y \end{pmatrix}, \qquad T = f'(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \\ y & x \end{pmatrix}. $$ By definition $L(T) = \max_{|H|=1}|TH|$. Put $H=(h,k)$ with $h=\cos\theta,k=\sin\theta$. Then $$ TH = \begin{pmatrix} h+k \\ h-k \\ hy+kx \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |TH|^2 = (h+k)^2+(h-k)^2+(hy+kx)^2 = 2(h^2+k^2) + (hy+kx)^2. $$ We maximize/minimize this under $h^2+k^2=1$, so $|TH|^2 = 2 + h^2y^2 + k^2x^2 + 2xyhk$.

Simplification

Substituting $h=\cos\theta,k=\sin\theta$: $$ |TH|^2 = 2 + y^2\cos^2\theta + x^2\sin^2\theta + 2xy\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2 + \frac{x^2+y^2}{2} + \frac{y^2-x^2}{2}\cos2\theta + xy\sin2\theta. $$ Let $x=r\cos\alpha$, $y=r\sin\alpha$, so $x^2+y^2=r^2$, $y^2-x^2 = -r^2\cos2\alpha$, $2xy=r^2\sin2\alpha$, giving $$ |TH|^2 = 2 + \frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^2}{2}\cos2(\alpha+\theta), $$ with $\cos 2(\alpha+\theta)$ ranging over $[-1,1]$.

When $\cos2(\alpha+\theta)=1$: $|TH|^2 = 2$, so $\ell(f'(x)) = \sqrt2$.
When $\cos2(\alpha+\theta)=-1$: $|TH|^2 = 2+r^2 = 2+x^2+y^2$, so $L(f'(x)) = \sqrt{2+x^2+y^2}$.

Verification that $L_f(x) = L(f'(x))$

By definition $L_f(x) = \limsup_{|H|\to 0} \dfrac{|f(x+h,y+k)-f(x,y)|}{|(h,k)|}$. Writing $K$ for this ratio, $$ K^2 = \frac{(h+k)^2+(h-k)^2+(xk+hy+kh)^2}{h^2+k^2} = 2 + \frac{(xk+hy+kh)^2}{h^2+k^2}. $$ Writing the increment in polar form $h=t\cos\theta$, $k=t\sin\theta$, $t=|(h,k)|\to 0$, $$ K^2 = 2 + \big(x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta + t\sin\theta\cos\theta\big)^2 = 2 + (x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta)^2 + \underbrace{t^2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta + 2x t\sin^2\theta\cos\theta + 2yt\sin\theta\cos^2\theta}_{\text{all carry a factor } t}. $$ The last group is bounded by $t^2 + 2|x|t + 2|y|t \to 0$ as $t\to 0$, uniformly in $\theta$, so the supremum over $\theta$ and the limit $t\to 0$ may be interchanged: $$ K^2 \to 2 + (x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta)^2. $$ With $x=r\cos\alpha,\,y=r\sin\alpha$, $x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta = r\sin(\theta+\alpha)$, so $$ K^2 \to 2 + r^2\sin^2(\theta+\alpha), $$ exactly the expression found above, since $2+\tfrac{r^2}{2}-\tfrac{r^2}{2}\cos2(\alpha+\theta) = 2+r^2\sin^2(\alpha+\theta)$. Taking the supremum and infimum over $\theta$ gives $$ L_f(x) = \sqrt{2+x^2+y^2} = L(f'(x)), \qquad \ell_f(x) = \sqrt2 = \ell(f'(x)), $$ in accordance with Theorem 4.1. $\blacksquare$
