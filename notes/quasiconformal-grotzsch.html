A. Dilatation

In the previous notes we found: let $f : A \to \mathbb{C}$ with $A \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, differentiable at $z$. Then $$ L_f(z) = |\partial f(z)| + |\bar\partial f(z)|, \qquad \ell_f(z) = \big||\partial f(z)| - |\bar\partial f(z)|\big|. $$ The obvious question: can we define the same notion of dilatation here as for a linear transformation? The answer is yes — but first, some preliminary quantities.

Let $f$ be differentiable at $z$ and sense-preserving (or sense-reversing). From $df = f_z\,dz + f_{\bar z}\,d\bar z$, it follows that $$ |dw| = |f_z\,dz + f_{\bar z}\,d\bar z| \le \big(|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|\big)|dz| \qquad (\because |dz|=|d\bar z|). $$ So, by the triangle inequality, $$ \big(|f_z|-|f_{\bar z}|\big)|dz| \;\le\; |dw| \;\le\; \big(|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|\big)|dz|. $$ We also have an easy way to compute $f_z$ and $f_{\bar z}$: $$ f_z = \tfrac12(u_x+v_y) + \tfrac{i}{2}(v_x-u_y), \qquad f_{\bar z} = \tfrac12(u_x-v_y) + \tfrac{i}{2}(v_x+u_y), $$ from which $|f_z|^2 - |f_{\bar z}|^2 = u_xv_y - u_yv_x = J$, the Jacobian. Since $f$ is sense-preserving, $J>0$, so $$ |f_z|^2 - |f_{\bar z}|^2 > 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; |f_z|^2 > |f_{\bar z}|^2 \ge 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; |f_z| > |f_{\bar z}| \ge 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; |f_z|-|f_{\bar z}| > 0. $$ So we are now in a position to define the dilatation of a complex $f$.

Definition Let $f:A\to\mathbb{C}$, $A\subseteq\mathbb{C}$, differentiable at $z$ and sense-preserving (or sense-reversing). The dilatation at a point $z_0$ is the ratio of maximal to minimal stretching at $z_0$: $$ D_f = \left|\frac{dw}{dz}\right| = \frac{|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|}{|f_z|-|f_{\bar z}|} \;\ge\; 1. $$

Definition of Quasiconformal Maps

Given the dilatation $D_f = \dfrac{|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|}{|f_z|-|f_{\bar z}|} \ge 1$, we can now say precisely what it means for a map to distort angles by only a bounded amount.

Definition Let $f : \Omega \to \Omega'$ be an orientation-preserving homeomorphism between domains $\Omega,\Omega' \subset \mathbb{C}$. We say $f$ is $K$-quasiconformal (for a constant $K \ge 1$) if $D_f(z) \le K$ for every $z \in \Omega$. We say $f$ is quasiconformal if it is $K$-quasiconformal for some finite $K$.

Equivalently, the image ellipse at $z$ has semi-major axis $|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|$ and semi-minor axis $|f_z|-|f_{\bar z}|$, and $D_f(z)$ is exactly the ratio of these axes.

  • $D_f \equiv 1$ (i.e. $K=1$) iff $f_{\bar z} \equiv 0$, i.e. $f$ is holomorphic — a $1$-quasiconformal map is precisely a conformal map.
  • Larger $K$ allows more distortion; a $K$-quasiconformal map is also $K'$-quasiconformal for any $K' \ge K$.

B. Grötzsch's Problem

Given two rectangles $R$ and $R'$ with different side ratios, there is in general no conformal map of $R$ onto $R'$ taking the $a$-sides to the $a$-sides and $b$-sides to $b$-sides (a conformal map can only do this when $a:b = a':b'$).

Problem (Grötzsch, 1928) If $Q$ is a square and $R$ is a rectangle, not a square, there is no conformal mapping of $Q$ onto $R$ sending vertices to vertices. Instead, Grötzsch asked for the most nearly conformal mapping of this kind. This calls for a measure of approximate conformality, and in supplying one, Grötzsch took the first step toward the creation of a theory of quasiconformal mappings.

Solution. We give Grötzsch's problem a precise meaning by saying $f$ is most nearly conformal if $\sup D_f$ is as small as possible.

Let $R,R'$ be rectangles with sides $a,b$ and $a',b'$. Assume $a:b \le a':b'$ (otherwise swap $a,b$). The mapping $f$ takes $a$-sides into $a$-sides and $b$-sides into $b$-sides.

We estimate $a'$ along a horizontal segment: $$ a' = \left|\int_0^a df(x+iy)\right| \le \int_0^a |df(x+iy)| \le \int_0^a \big(|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|\big)\,dx. $$ Since $b = \int_0^b dy$, $$ a'b \le \int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b \big(|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|\big)\,dx\,dy. $$ Squaring both sides, $$ a'^2 b^2 \le \left(\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b \big(|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|\big)\,dx\,dy\right)^2. $$ Now bound the right side via Hölder's inequality: $\big(\int |fg|\,dx\big)^2 \le \int|f|^2dx \cdot \int|g|^2 dx$. Apply this with $f(x,y) = \sqrt{\dfrac{|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|}{|f_z|-|f_{\bar z}|}}$ and $g(x,y) = \sqrt{|f_z|^2-|f_{\bar z}|^2}$, so $fg = |f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|$. Then $$ \left(\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b |f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|\,dx\,dy\right)^2 \le \int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b \frac{|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|}{|f_z|-|f_{\bar z}|}\,dx\,dy \cdot \int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b \big(|f_z|^2-|f_{\bar z}|^2\big)\,dx\,dy. $$ Hence $$ a'^2 b^2 \le \int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b D_f\,dx\,dy \cdot \int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b J\,dx\,dy = \int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b D_f\,dx\,dy \cdot a'b', $$ (the area integral of the Jacobian over $R$ is the area $a'b'$ of the image). Dividing through, $$ \frac{a'^2 b^2}{a'b'ab} \le \frac{1}{ab}\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b D_f\,dx\,dy, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \frac{a'/b'}{a/b} \le \frac{1}{ab}\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b D_f\,dx\,dy. $$ If $f$ is $K$-quasiconformal, the right side is at most $\dfrac{K}{ab}\displaystyle\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^b dx\,dy = K$. So $$ \frac{a'/b'}{a/b} \le K. $$ The ratios $m=a/b$ and $m'=a'/b'$ are the moduli of $R$ and $R'$. There exists some $f$ attaining the least maximum, i.e. $\sup D_f = m'/m$. We have proved:

Result There exists a $K$-quasiconformal mapping of $R$ onto $R'$ if and only if $\dfrac{1}{K} \le \dfrac{m'}{m} \le K$.

Examples

These three examples illustrate quasiconformal mapping from a unit square $R=[0,1]\times[0,1]$ to a rectangle $R'=[0,4]\times[0,1]$, together with the geometry behind each map.

1. The affine map (the optimum)

Let $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$, $f(x,y) = (4x,y)$. In complex form: $$ f(z) = \tfrac52 z + \tfrac32 \bar z, \qquad D_f \equiv 4. $$ This is exactly the affine form $f(z)=\alpha z+\beta\bar z$ with $\alpha=\tfrac52,\beta=\tfrac32$ real and constant. Every image ellipse is the same circle-turned-$4{:}1$-ellipse: $\operatorname{avg}_R D_f = \sup_R D_f = 4$. This is the theoretical minimum, attained.

2. A non-affine competitor

Let $f(x,y) = \big(4x,\ \tfrac32 y - \tfrac12 y^2\big)$. With $a'/a = 4$ and $b'/b = \tfrac12(3-2y)$, $$ f(z) = \Big(\tfrac{11}{4} - \tfrac{y}{2}\Big) z + \Big(\tfrac54+\tfrac{y}{2}\Big)\bar z, \qquad D_f = \frac{4}{\tfrac32 - y} = \frac{8}{3-2y}. $$ The quadratic term shows this map is genuinely non-affine — not of the form $\alpha z+\beta\bar z$ — consistent with $D_f$ depending on $y$. Ellipses flatten going up: $D_f$ runs from $\tfrac83 \approx 2.67$ at $y=0$ (better than the affine map!) up to $8$ at $y=1$ (twice as bad). $$ \operatorname{avg}_R D_f = \int_0^1 \frac{4}{\tfrac32-y}\,dy = 4\ln 3 \approx 4.39 \ge 4, \qquad \sup_R D_f = 8 \ge 4. $$ Being locally better forces a worse maximum — this map loses.

3. A degenerate competitor

Let $f(x,y) = (4x, y^2)$. Then $f_z = \tfrac12(4+2y)$, $f_{\bar z} = \tfrac12(4-2y)$, so $$ f(z) = \tfrac12(4+2y)z + \tfrac12(4-2y)\bar z, \qquad D_f = \frac{\tfrac12(4+2y)+\tfrac12(4-2y)}{\tfrac12(4+2y)-\tfrac12(4-2y)} = \frac{4}{2y} = \frac{2}{y}. $$ Ellipses collapse toward the bottom edge, where $2y\to 0$: $$ \operatorname{avg}_R D_f = \int_0^1 \frac{2}{y}\,dy = \infty, \qquad \sup_R D_f = \infty. $$ The bound $\sup D_f \ge 4$ holds vacuously — this competitor loses badly.

MapReal form $(u,v)$Complex form $f(z)$
1. Affine$(4x,\,y)$$\tfrac52 z + \tfrac32 \bar z$
2. Competitor$\big(4x,\,\tfrac32y-\tfrac12y^2\big)$$\big(\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac{y}2\big)z + \big(\tfrac54+\tfrac{y}2\big)\bar z$
3. Degenerate$(4x,\,y^2)$$\tfrac12(4+2y)z + \tfrac12(4-2y)\bar z$

Only Example 1 is affine — linear in $z,\bar z$ with no higher powers. Examples 2 and 3 both carry a variable coefficient, since both have a $y^2$ piece; differentiating it reintroduces $y$-dependence in $f_z,f_{\bar z}$, which is exactly why $D_f$ varies with $y$ in those two cases but not the first. All three satisfy $\iint_R J_f = 4 = \operatorname{area}(R')$, confirming they are genuine competitors onto the same target. Only the affine map attains $\sup D_f = m'/m = 4$ — it is the unique minimiser.

Additional quasiconformal examples

These examples illustrate other important classes of quasiconformal mappings, not restricted to the fixed square-to-rectangle problem above.

4. Global $K$-quasiconformal stretching

Consider $f(z) = x+iKy$, $z=x+iy$, $K\ge 1$. In real coordinates $u=x,\ v=Ky$, so $Df = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&K\end{pmatrix}$. The complex derivatives are $f_z = \tfrac12(1+K)$, $f_{\bar z}=\tfrac12(1-K)$, so $$ D_f = \frac{|f_z|+|f_{\bar z}|}{|f_z|-|f_{\bar z}|} = K \qquad\text{(constant)}. $$ The Jacobian is $J_f = K > 0$, so $f$ is globally $K$-quasiconformal. Geometrically, the vertical direction is stretched by $K$ while the horizontal is unchanged — a circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axis ratio $K$.

5. Spiral stretching

Consider the linear map $f(z) = x + i(1-ci)y$, $c\in\mathbb{R}$. Since $i(1-ci) = c+i$, this is $f(z) = x+cy+iy$, so $u=x+cy,\ v=y$, and $Df = \begin{pmatrix}1&c\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ — a shear. The complex derivatives are $f_z = 1-\tfrac{ic}2$, $f_{\bar z} = \tfrac{ic}2$, so $|f_z| = \sqrt{1+c^2/4}$, $|f_{\bar z}| = |c|/2$, giving $$ D_f = \frac{\sqrt{c^2+4}+|c|}{\sqrt{c^2+4}-|c|}. $$ The Jacobian is $J_f = |f_z|^2-|f_{\bar z}|^2 = 1$, so the map is orientation-preserving and quasiconformal for every finite $c$ — a shear that can be viewed as a spiral-type distortion of infinitesimal shapes. For $c=0$ the map is the identity and $D_f=1$.

6. Radial stretching

Consider $f(z) = z|z|^{\alpha-1}$, $\alpha>0$. Writing $z=re^{i\theta}$, $f(z) = r^\alpha e^{i\theta}$ — the radial coordinate transforms as $r\mapsto r^\alpha$ while the angle is preserved. For $z\ne0$, $$ f_z = \tfrac{\alpha+1}2|z|^{\alpha-1}, \qquad f_{\bar z} = \tfrac{\alpha-1}2|z|^{\alpha-1}\tfrac{z}{\bar z}, $$ so $|f_z| = \tfrac{\alpha+1}2|z|^{\alpha-1}$, $|f_{\bar z}| = \tfrac{|\alpha-1|}2|z|^{\alpha-1}$, giving $$ D_f = \frac{\alpha+1+|\alpha-1|}{\alpha+1-|\alpha-1|} = \max\Big\{\alpha,\ \frac1\alpha\Big\}. $$ In particular $D_f=\alpha$ for $\alpha\ge1$ and $D_f=1/\alpha$ for $0<\alpha\le1$. The Jacobian $J_f = \alpha|z|^{2\alpha-2}>0$ for $z\ne0$. At $\alpha=1$, $f$ is the identity and conformal ($D_f=1$). So radial stretching gives a simple example where the distortion is constant even though the amount of stretching depends on distance from the origin.

7. Radial stretching with logarithmic twisting

A more general example: $f(z) = z|z|^{\alpha-1+i\beta}$, $\alpha>0,\ \beta\in\mathbb{R}$. Since $|z|^{i\beta} = e^{i\beta\log|z|}$, $$ f(z) = |z|^\alpha e^{i(\theta+\beta\log|z|)}, $$ so in polar coordinates $r\mapsto r^\alpha$, $\theta\mapsto \theta+\beta\log r$: $\alpha$ controls radial stretching, $\beta$ controls logarithmic twisting. For $z\ne0$, $$ f_z = \tfrac{\alpha+1+i\beta}2|z|^{\alpha-1+i\beta}, \qquad f_{\bar z} = \tfrac{\alpha-1+i\beta}2|z|^{\alpha-1+i\beta}\tfrac{z}{\bar z}, $$ and since $|z^{i\beta}|=1$, $$ |f_z| = \frac{\sqrt{(\alpha+1)^2+\beta^2}}2|z|^{\alpha-1}, \qquad |f_{\bar z}| = \frac{\sqrt{(\alpha-1)^2+\beta^2}}2|z|^{\alpha-1}. $$ Therefore $$ D_f = \frac{\sqrt{(\alpha+1)^2+\beta^2}+\sqrt{(\alpha-1)^2+\beta^2}}{\sqrt{(\alpha+1)^2+\beta^2}-\sqrt{(\alpha-1)^2+\beta^2}}, $$ equivalently,

$$ D_f = \frac{\alpha^2+\beta^2+1+\sqrt{(\alpha^2+\beta^2+1)^2-4\alpha^2}}{2\alpha}. $$

The Jacobian is $J_f = \alpha|z|^{2\alpha-2} > 0$. Hence, for $\alpha>0$, this is an orientation-preserving quasiconformal mapping on $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$. When $\beta=0$, Example 7 reduces exactly to the radial stretching of Example 6: $f(z) = z|z|^{\alpha-1}$. When additionally $\alpha=1$, $f(z)=z|z|^{i\beta} = ze^{i\beta\log|z|}$, a pure logarithmic twist.

Mathematica code

The following code (used to generate the interactive version of the figures above) lets you visualize the distortion for different values of $\alpha$ and $\beta$:

ClearAll["Global`*"];

Manipulate[
  Module[{radii, angles, f, circles, radialLines, imageCircles, imageLines},

    f[z_] := z Abs[z]^(alpha - 1 + I beta);

    radii = Range[0.4, 2.5, 0.4];
    angles = Range[0, 2 Pi - Pi/12, Pi/12];

    circles = Table[
      ParametricPlot[{r Cos[t], r Sin[t]}, {t, 0, 2 Pi},
        PlotStyle -> Directive[Gray, Thin]], {r, radii}];

    radialLines = Table[
      ParametricPlot[{r Cos[theta], r Sin[theta]}, {r, 0.05, 2.5},
        PlotStyle -> Directive[Gray, Thin]], {theta, angles}];

    imageCircles = Table[
      ParametricPlot[
        With[{w = f[r Exp[I t]]}, {Re[w], Im[w]}],
        {t, 0, 2 Pi}, PlotStyle -> Directive[Blue, Thick]],
      {r, radii}];

    imageLines = Table[
      ParametricPlot[
        With[{w = f[r Exp[I theta]]}, {Re[w], Im[w]}],
        {r, 0.05, 2.5}, PlotStyle -> Directive[Red, Thick]],
      {theta, angles}];

    GraphicsGrid[{{Show[circles, radialLines,
        PlotRange -> {{-3, 3}, {-3, 3}}, AspectRatio -> 1,
        Axes -> True, PlotLabel -> "Original Grid"],

      Show[imageCircles, imageLines,
        PlotRange -> {{-5, 5}, {-5, 5}}, AspectRatio -> 1,
        Axes -> True,
        PlotLabel -> Row[{"Image under f(z) = z |z|^(\[Alpha]-1+i\[Beta])",
          "\n", "\[Alpha] = ", alpha, ", \[Beta] = ", beta}]]
    }}]],

  {{alpha, 1.5, "\[Alpha] (radial stretching)"}, 0.5, 2.5, 0.1,
    Appearance -> "Labeled"},

  {{beta, 0, "\[Beta] (logarithmic twist)"}, -2, 2, 0.05,
    Appearance -> "Labeled"},

  ControlPlacement -> Top
]
